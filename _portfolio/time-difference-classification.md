**Technical exploration · September 2026 · Google Earth Engine · Sentinel-2 SR Harmonized, 2024 · South Kalimantan, Indonesia**

## Research question

Does splitting a year into several shorter observation periods, classifying each one, and
combining the results produce a better land-cover map than classifying a single composite?

The honest answer from this script is: **it does not say yet.** The workflow builds the
comparison — three periods, one classifier, one combined output, and two disagreement
layers — but it contains no validation samples and no accuracy assessment, so nothing here
measures *better*. What it does produce is a measure of **stability**: how far the four
mapped classes move when the observation window moves. This page documents that design,
states plainly where it stops, and sets out what a version that answers the title question
would need.

## Why the question is worth asking

A land-cover map made from a single annual composite carries a hidden assumption: that one
summary of the year represents the surface well enough for every class. In the humid tropics
that assumption is strained from two directions at once. Persistent cloud means an annual
median is assembled from an uneven set of clear observations, and several of the classes
mapped here are not static — open land and shrubland in particular pass through cycles of
clearing, regrowth and drying that look spectrally different depending on when they are
observed.

Splitting the year is the standard response, and the case for it is well established: a
review of optical time-series methods for land-cover classification found that temporal
information consistently adds discriminating power over single-date imagery, particularly
for classes whose separability depends on how they change rather than on how they look at
one moment (Gómez et al., 2016). What is less often stated is *how much* it adds in a
specific landscape and class scheme, and whether the gain survives an honest accuracy
assessment. That is the version of the question this exploration is built towards.

## Study area and classes

The script defines the analysis extent by intersection rather than by hand. A multi-polygon
`roi` is filtered to the single geometry that contains the training points, which keeps the
analysis inside the area the labels actually describe:

```js
var studyArea = roi.geometries().map(function(g) {
  var intersect = ee.Geometry(g).intersects(allSamples.geometry());
  return ee.Algorithms.If(intersect, g, null);
}, true).get(0);
```

Four classes are mapped, labelled through an `lc` property on four FeatureCollections:

| `lc` | Class | Map colour |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Forest | dark green |
| 2 | Bareland | olive |
| 3 | Open Land | yellow |
| 4 | Shrubland | bright green |

## Data and temporal design

The optical input is `COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED` surface reflectance for 2024 (Google
Earth Engine, n.d.), restricted to scenes with `CLOUDY_PIXEL_PERCENTAGE` below 30 and
divided into three four-month periods:

| Period | Window |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 January – 30 April 2024 |
| 2 | 1 May – 31 August 2024 |
| 3 | 1 September – 31 December 2024 |

Each period is reduced to a median composite and clipped to the study geometry. The
windows are equal in length, but not in content: cloud cover is not distributed evenly
across a tropical year, so the number of clear observations contributing to each median
differs, and a composite assembled from few scenes is not the same kind of summary as one
assembled from many.

Masking uses the Scene Classification Layer, removing saturated or defective pixels (SCL 1),
cloud shadows (3), medium- and high-probability cloud (8, 9) and cirrus (10). Reflectance is
then rescaled by dividing by 10,000, and six bands are kept: B2, B3, B4, B8, B11, B12.

Three SCL classes are left unmasked and are worth naming, because each can put non-surface
values into a median: dark-area pixels (2), low-probability cloud and unclassified (7), and
snow or ice (11). The first two matter most here — residual haze and unflagged shadow
survive into the composite rather than being excluded from it.

As on the Sentinel-2 side of the [AlphaEarth comparison]({{ site.baseurl }}/portfolio/s2-alphaearth-embeddings-land-cover/),
the feature stack is presented at 10 m but is not uniformly 10 m information: B2, B3, B4 and
B8 are native 10 m bands, while B11 and B12 are native 20 m bands resampled to the sampling
scale.

## Feature construction

Each period is unmixed against five fixed spectral endmembers — green vegetation, shade,
non-photosynthetic vegetation, soil and cloud — under sum-to-one and non-negativity
constraints. Spectral mixture analysis treats a pixel as a linear combination of a small
number of pure surface components, so the output is the proportion of the pixel each
component accounts for rather than its reflectance:

$$ \rho_b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i \, \rho_{i,b} + \varepsilon_b, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i = 1, \qquad f_i \geq 0 $$

where $$\rho_b$$ is observed reflectance in band $$b$$, $$\rho_{i,b}$$ is the reflectance of
endmember $$i$$ in that band, $$f_i$$ is the fraction of the pixel attributed to it and
$$\varepsilon_b$$ the residual. Classifying on fractions rather than on raw reflectance is a
long-established way to make land-cover categories comparable across scenes with different
illumination and mixing conditions (Adams et al., 1995) — which is exactly the situation
when three composites from different parts of a year are put side by side.

The endmembers are hard-coded generic spectra, not derived from these images. That makes the
script portable, and it means the fractions are internally consistent across the three
periods — the same reference spectra are used each time — but it also means they are not
calibrated to this landscape. A fraction of 0.6 soil here is a position in a fixed mixing
space, not a measured 60 % soil cover.

### A note on the index named NDFI

The script derives one further band from the fractions:

$$ \text{script index} = \frac{GV - NPV}{GV + NPV} $$

It is labelled `NDFI`, but this is not the Normalized Difference Fraction Index as published.
Souza et al. (2005) define NDFI on the shade-normalised green-vegetation fraction and against
the **sum** of the non-photosynthetic vegetation and soil fractions:

$$ NDFI = \frac{GV_{shade} - (NPV + Soil)}{GV_{shade} + NPV + Soil}, \qquad GV_{shade} = \frac{GV}{1 - Shade} $$

Two differences matter. Omitting shade normalisation leaves the index sensitive to
illumination and canopy shadowing, which is the specific problem the normalisation exists to
remove. Omitting the soil fraction from the denominator changes what the index contrasts:
the published form separates photosynthetic vegetation from *all* non-photosynthetic
surface, whereas the form here separates it from dry vegetation only, leaving bare soil out
of the comparison — a consequential omission when two of the four target classes are
Bareland and Open Land.

The band is a perfectly usable normalised difference of two fractions; the issue is the
name. **NEEDS USER CONFIRMATION:** whether to rename it (`GV_NPV_ratio` would describe it
accurately) or to implement the published formula. Until one or the other is done, the label
invites readers to assume a well-known index that the script does not compute. The
description on this page deliberately refers to it by what it calculates.

The twelve predictors passed to the classifier are the six reflectance bands, the five
fractions and that index.

## Classification design

One Random Forest is trained — 150 trees, three variables per split, a minimum leaf
population of five, seed 42 — and applied to all three periods.

This is the single most important thing to understand about the design, because it is easy
to misread. The classifier is trained on **period 1 only**:

```js
var training = images[0].select(predictorBands).sampleRegions({
  collection: trainingPoints, properties: ['lc'], scale: 10, tileScale: 16
});
```

The three classifications are therefore not three independent models. They are one model,
fitted to January–April conditions, then transferred to May–August and September–December.
That makes the design a clean test of one thing and a confounded test of another. It cleanly
isolates the **image** as the variable: the model is held constant, so differences between
the three maps come from the imagery. But it means any difference between periods mixes
together three causes that the script cannot separate — real change on the ground, seasonal
and illumination differences in how an unchanged surface appears, and the error incurred by
applying a model outside the conditions it was fitted to.

Every training feature is buffered by 30 m before sampling. Where the labels are points —
as the script's own comments describe them — that turns each label into roughly 28 pixels at
10 m rather than one. Those pixels are adjacent and strongly correlated, so the buffer
enlarges the training table without adding a corresponding amount of independent
information — worth remembering when the model appears well supported.

The three classifications are then combined per pixel:

- **`FinalClass`** — the modal class across the three periods. Where all three agree this
  changes nothing; where one period dissents it is outvoted.
- **`Variance`** — the variance of the three class values, offered as a consistency layer.
- **`Change_Detected`** — where period 1 and period 3 disagree.

### Why the variance layer needs care

`ee.Reducer.variance()` operates on the class codes 1–4 as if they were quantities, but they
are nominal labels: Forest is not one unit less than Bareland. A pixel classified Forest (1)
twice and Shrubland (4) once produces a much larger variance than one classified Forest (1)
twice and Bareland (2) once, although both describe exactly one dissenting period. The layer
therefore ranks disagreement partly by how far apart the class codes happen to be, which is
an artefact of the numbering rather than a property of the landscape.

Two alternatives fit nominal data. The count of distinct labels across the three periods
gives 1, 2 or 3 and is directly interpretable. The number of periods agreeing with the modal
class gives 3, 2 or — where all three differ — 1, and doubles as a confidence layer for
`FinalClass`. Either would say what the variance layer is reaching for, without depending on
the arbitrary ordering of the class codes.

Random Forest is used because it accepts many correlated predictors without prior feature
selection and makes no assumption about their distribution or units (Breiman, 2001) — here
reflectance, mixture fractions and a normalised index are fed to the same estimator without
rescaling. It classifies a pixel by majority vote across its trees:

$$ \hat{y}(x) = \operatorname{mode}\{ T_1(x), T_2(x), \ldots, T_B(x) \} $$

with $$B = 150$$. The majority vote across the three periods applies the same idea one level
up — a vote over images rather than over trees. The whole workflow runs in the Earth Engine
Code Editor (Gorelick et al., 2017).

## Outputs

The script produces six map layers and two charts:

1. three single-period classifications, one per four-month window;
2. `FinalClass`, the majority vote across them;
3. the variance layer, discussed above;
4. the period 1 versus period 3 change layer;
5. a Random Forest variable-importance chart, from `classifier.explain()`; and
6. a grouped column chart of class area in hectares for each period, computed with
   `ee.Image.pixelArea()` and a grouped sum reducer.

`FinalClass` is exported to Drive at 10 m.

No exported output was available when this page was written, so no classified maps, no
importance ranking and no area figures are reproduced here. Nothing is claimed about which
classes moved between periods or by how much — those are empirical results, and they will be
added only once the exports exist.

A wording note on the charts: the area chart labels the three windows *Season 1*, *Season 2*
and *Season 3*. Four-month calendar blocks are not seasons in any climatological sense, and
this page calls them periods throughout. Renaming the chart labels would make the script
consistent with that.

## What the design can and cannot show

**It can show stability.** Three maps of the same place, made by the same model from
different parts of one year, can be compared directly. Where they agree, the mapped class is
robust to the choice of observation window. Where they disagree, either the surface changed
or the class is sensitive to when it was observed — and the area chart shows how far each
class total moves between periods.

**It cannot show accuracy.** There is no reference dataset, no train/test separation and no
confusion matrix. Agreement between periods is not correctness: three periods can agree on
the same wrong label, and a majority vote over three correlated errors produces a confident
wrong answer. The `FinalClass` layer is smoother than any single-period map, and smoother is
easy to mistake for better.

**It cannot attribute change.** The change layer marks where period 1 and period 3 differ.
It does not say whether that difference is clearing, regrowth, a phenological swing, residual
cloud in one composite, or the transferred classifier failing on period 3 conditions it never
saw in training.

So the value of the exercise is methodological. It isolates the observation window as a
variable, holds the model constant, and produces exactly the layers a validated experiment
would then measure — which is the necessary step before the title question can be answered,
and not the answer itself.

## Limitations

- No independent validation dataset, no hold-out separation, no confusion matrix. No
  accuracy figure is reported or implied anywhere on this page.
- The classifier is trained on period 1 and transferred to periods 2 and 3, so
  between-period differences confound real change, seasonal appearance and transfer error.
- The variance layer applies a numeric reducer to nominal class codes; its magnitude depends
  on the class numbering.
- The band labelled `NDFI` is not NDFI as published by Souza et al. (2005).
- Endmembers are fixed generic spectra, not derived from these images, so the fractions are
  relative positions in a fixed mixing space rather than calibrated cover proportions.
- A cloud fraction is both masked upstream (SCL) and modelled as an endmember, and is then
  used as a classification predictor — residual haze can therefore influence the class
  decision rather than only being excluded from it.
- Training points are buffered to 30 m before sampling at 10 m, so training pixels are
  spatially autocorrelated and the effective sample size is smaller than the table suggests.
- SCL classes 2, 7 and 11 are not masked.
- B11 and B12 are native 20 m bands used at a 10 m sampling scale.
- The three composites draw on different numbers of clear scenes, so they are not equally
  well supported summaries of their windows.
- Only four classes are mapped, in one study area. Nothing here generalises to other class
  schemes or landscapes without further testing.

## What would answer the title question

Turning this design into an experiment that measures improvement needs four additions.

**A reference sample drawn independently of the training labels**, stratified by mapped
class, with the sample size and allocation chosen in advance — and area and accuracy
estimated with the unbiased estimators and confidence intervals set out by Olofsson et al.
(2014), so that a difference between two maps can be distinguished from sampling noise.

**A like-for-like set of competitors**: a single annual composite classified once, each
four-month period classified on its own, and the majority-vote composite — all assessed
against the same reference sample. Without the annual baseline there is nothing for the
multi-period result to improve *on*.

**Separation of the two variables now entangled.** Training on all three periods, rather
than on period 1 alone, turns the transfer error into a measurable term instead of a
confound; comparing that against the current period-1-only model shows how much of any
difference was transfer error all along.

**Per-class reporting.** An overall accuracy can hide the result that matters. If splitting
the year helps Open Land and Shrubland — the classes whose appearance changes within a year
— while leaving Forest and Bareland unchanged, that is a more useful finding than a single
number, and it is the finding the temporal-classification literature would predict
(Gómez et al., 2016).

Spatial cross-validation would guard against the optimism that follows from training and
testing on adjacent pixels, which the 30 m buffers make a live concern here.

## Related work

Readers following this question further may find the following relevant. Each is listed for
what it contributes to the specific problem above, not as general background.

**On multi-period classification in Indonesia.** Splitting a year into periods and
classifying each is the approach taken in
[Mapping Paddy Field Extent and Temporal Pattern Variation in a Complex Terrain Area](https://doi.org/10.52939/ijg.v17i2.1763)
(Arjasakusuma et al., 2021), where twelve monthly Sentinel-1 composites were used to
separate paddy field from other cover in Magelang, and the temporal pattern itself — not
the appearance at any one date — carried the cropping-frequency information. That study also
reports what the present script does not: an accuracy assessment, with the change in user's
and producer's accuracy attributed to a specific added variable. The radar equivalent of the
design on this page is in my portfolio as
[Object-Based Crop Classification from Multi-Period Sentinel-1 Time Series]({{ site.baseurl }}/portfolio/s1-object-based-crop-classification/),
which splits 2023 into three periods.

**On the validation this page is missing.** The reason the title question stays open here is
the absence of a reference sample, and how that sample is drawn is not a detail:
[Understanding the influence of different sample sizes and sample techniques on accuracy assessment of land cover mapping](https://doi.org/10.1117/12.3009445)
(Mahendra & Danoedoro, 2024) tests how far accuracy estimates move with sample size and sampling
technique for a 25- and a 9-class scheme in Salatiga. Its portfolio page is
[Sampling Design and Accuracy Assessment for Land-Cover Mapping]({{ site.baseurl }}/portfolio/salatiga-sampling-accuracy/).
That work is the direct prerequisite for the experiment described in the section above.

**On multi-temporal change detection.** For the change-layer side of the workflow,
[Mangroves change detection using support vector machine algorithm on Google Earth Engine](https://acrs-aars.org/proceeding/ACRS2019/ThC1-3.pdf)
(Mahendra et al., 2019) applies annual classification and comparison across 2014–2018 in the
Gulf of Bone, and runs into the same interpretive problem named above: separating genuine
cover change from the spectral influence of water and sediment background.

**On what a long multi-temporal record supports.**
[Codification to secure Indonesian peatlands](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soisec.2022.100080)
(Widyatmanti et al., 2022) uses land-use and land-cover maps at five-year steps from 1990 to
2020 to relate change to the timing of peatland regulation — an illustration of what
consistent multi-period classification is ultimately *for*, once the accuracy of each map has
been established.

**On the feature representation.** The companion exploration
[Comparing Sentinel-2 Spectral Features and AlphaEarth Satellite Embeddings]({{ site.baseurl }}/portfolio/s2-alphaearth-embeddings-land-cover/)
holds the observation window constant and varies the features instead — the complementary
experiment to this one.
